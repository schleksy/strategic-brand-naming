## Name types, research territories, and construction methods

These are different axes. A name can be suggestive, come from a historical territory, and use compression. Do not confuse "abstract" with "meaningless" or let a taxonomy become a quota.

| Name type | What it does | Main tradeoff to test |
|---|---|---|
| Descriptive | States an offering, function, or outcome directly | Clarity versus differentiation and expansion |
| Suggestive / evocative | Hints at a benefit, behavior, or feeling | Relevance without excessive explanation |
| Metaphorical | Borrows an analogous object or system | Whether the analogy actually maps |
| Arbitrary / abstract | Uses a word whose familiar meaning is outside the category | Memorability versus initial context needed |
| Coined / invented | Creates a new word, with or without semantic roots | Distinctiveness versus pronunciation and spelling friction |
| Founder / eponymous | Uses a person's name | Identity, associations, permission, and future transfer |
| Geographic / cultural | Uses a place, tradition, or culturally grounded reference | Authenticity, baggage, and expansion |
| Acronym / initialism | Compresses a phrase into letters | Recall, spoken form, ambiguity, and meaning loss |

Compounds, blends, altered spelling, compression, alliteration, rhythm, and double meanings are construction choices that can support several name types. Historical, linguistic, cultural, functional, and consequence research are territories from which those choices emerge.

## Research depth and order

Use these tiers as escalating depth, not a requirement to exhaust every language or obscure reference.

| Tier | Investigation | Evidence/output |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Brand and category | User behavior, audience, outcome, competitors, clichés, desired associations | Brief, exclusions, live landscape map |
| 2. Lateral systems | Consequences, metaphors, objects, professions, cultural practices | Several genuinely different territories |
| 3. Deep origins | History, people, literature, mythology, regional language, etymology | Source-backed root/story cards with pronunciation and baggage |
| 4. Construction | Compounds, coinage, compression, phonetic and visual variants | Candidate pool tied to roots, without fabricated derivations |
| 5. Qualification | Intrinsic knockout, then fresh company/domain/trademark/language/social checks | Dated evidence, eliminated names, screened shortlist |

Verify an unusual historical or linguistic claim before building a persuasive story on it. Do not postpone all fact checking until the final shortlist. The later screening phase checks the surviving name's market and linguistic usability in greater depth.

## Core Principle

Naming is not a brainstorm.

The job is not to produce a clever list of names. The job is to investigate the brand from enough different angles that unusually strong naming ideas can emerge, then eliminate almost everything until only strategically strong, usable candidates remain.

Do not keep producing variations of a weak naming direction. When a territory stops producing strong work, diagnose why and move to a genuinely different territory.

Discovery, screening, and selection are separate stages.

## Phase 1: understand the brand

Before generating names, complete [intake.md](intake.md). Establish the actual business, industry/subsector, offering and differentiation, business model, audience/buyer, markets, desired associations, future scope, selected name types, and domain requirements. Explicitly ask which name types to prioritize and avoid using the menu above. Accept an explicit open mix; do not choose silently.

Use context already available. Ask grouped material questions and wait for missing core answers. Do not convert an unanswered requirement into a provisional assumption to skip intake. An explicit request for immediate exploratory ideas permits a clearly labeled exception, not a completed recommendation.

Determine:

- What exactly is being named?
- Company, product, service, platform, feature, initiative, holding company, etc.
- What does it actually do?
- What behavior/problem/outcome matters most?
- Who is the audience?
- Who pays?
- Who uses it?
- Who needs to trust it?
- What industry, subsector, and market/category does it compete in?
- What is the business model and positioning?
- What makes the offering different from the main alternatives?
- Which name types should be prioritized or excluded?
- What should the name make someone feel?
- What should it absolutely not feel like?
- Should the meaning be immediately apparent or discoverable later?
- Should it sound established, premium, aggressive, playful, technical, human, institutional, cultural, etc.?
- How broad could the company become?
- Does the name need room for future products?
- Geographic markets and languages
- Required domain strategy, especially whether exact .com is mandatory
- Trademark jurisdictions that matter
- Length constraints
- Pronunciation constraints
- Spelling constraints
- Existing naming conventions
- Competitor naming patterns
- Names/styles the founder likes
- Names/styles the founder rejects

Do not ask questions already answered by prior context.

### Strategic test

Define:

1. What does winning look like?
2. What does the company already have going for it?
3. What does the company need the name to contribute?
4. What should the name communicate, imply, or deliberately leave unsaid?

The name is a strategic tool, not a description field.

## Phase 2: map the naming landscape

Before creating actual names, research the category using [research-sources.md](research-sources.md). Read actual company/product and industry sources. For a full naming project, compare genuinely different territories, including a lateral territory, with depth driven by the brief and evidence rather than a fixed quota. Record source-backed root cards before constructing names; a list of territory labels is not research.

Study:

- direct competitors
- adjacent industries
- naming conventions
- overused words
- suffix/prefix trends
- common metaphor territories
- category clichés
- heavily trademarked language
- semantic white space
- names that visually or phonetically blend together

The goal is to understand what **not** to sound like as much as what to sound like.

If every competitor uses words like Signal, Pulse, AI, Social, Guard, Watch, Labs, etc., those become reasons to explore elsewhere rather than ingredients for another compound.

## Phase 3: build distinct naming territories

Never begin with one semantic direction.

Build multiple genuinely different territories.

A territory is an idea-world, not a list of synonyms.

### Territory A: Direct behavior / problem

Investigate the actual action occurring.

Examples of questions:

- What is the user doing?
- What is the problem doing?
- What verb describes the behavior?
- What happens immediately before or after it?
- What does the company prevent, detect, enable, remove, reveal, or accelerate?

Explore terminology, slang, professional vocabulary, older vocabulary, verbs, nouns, idioms, and behavioral language.

Do not assume the obvious keyword itself should become the name.

---

### Territory B: Outcome / consequence

Move from what the product does to what happens because of it.

Research:

- consequences
- threats
- benefits
- secondary effects
- escalation
- protection
- momentum
- exposure
- damage
- recovery
- amplification
- suppression
- certainty
- discovery

This often produces stronger metaphors than direct feature language.

---

### Territory C: Metaphor

Ask what the problem resembles in another system.

Investigate unrelated domains such as:

- weather
- fire
- pressure
- navigation
- medicine
- biology
- physics
- geology
- military warning systems
- journalism
- signal detection
- surveillance
- acoustics
- optics
- law
- transportation
- architecture
- crowds
- ecosystems
- contamination
- chain reactions

The metaphor must map naturally to the product.

Do not select a metaphor merely because the word sounds cool.

A good metaphor creates a complete brand story.

---

### Territory D: History / people / archetypes

Research people, events, objects, systems, practices, myths, literature, folklore, and historical concepts connected to the underlying behavior.

Look for:

- a person whose name became synonymous with the behavior
- an old institution that performed an analogous function
- an historical object used for the same purpose
- a mythological figure associated with the behavior
- a literary character whose defining trait maps to the problem
- an old cultural practice that resembles the modern product

The ideal result has an origin story that makes people say:

> “Oh, that’s why it’s called that.”

Research historical examples appropriate to the current project. Do not permanently reuse a previous project's historical references. Every project requires new research.

---

### Territory E: Language and etymology

Investigate how other languages and older language families express the core idea.

Potential sources:

- Latin
- Greek
- Old English
- Romance languages
- Germanic languages
- Hebrew when relevant
- culturally relevant regional languages
- technical etymology
- extinct or historical forms where appropriate

Research:

- literal meaning
- root construction
- secondary meanings
- pronunciation
- spelling
- native-speaker associations
- slang
- offensive meanings
- existing brand use

Foreign-language research is not permission to grab an exotic-looking word.

The linguistic story must be accurate.

---

### Territory F: Cultural language

Investigate language native to the audience or behavior.

This may include:

- internet culture
- industry slang
- professional jargon
- journalism
- legal terminology
- creator vocabulary
- community-specific phrases
- generational language
- old terminology that has regained relevance

Avoid names that become dated because they depend entirely on current slang.

---

### Territory G: Objects / symbols / systems

Find something physical or conceptual that performs a similar role.

Ask:

- What detects this?
- What warns about this?
- What records it?
- What contains it?
- What exposes it?
- What amplifies it?
- What leaves evidence of it?
- What appears before it happens?
- What protects against it?

This can create arbitrary or suggestive names with stronger long-term flexibility.

---

### Territory H: Linguistic construction

Only after establishing strong semantic material should word construction begin.

Explore:

- compounds
- blends
- altered spellings
- compression
- dropped letters
- shortened roots
- suffix changes
- prefix changes
- phonetic variants
- semantic hybrids
- coined words derived from meaningful roots
- double meanings
- controlled wordplay

#### Compression rule

Compression must improve the brand.

Removing letters can damage instant readability even when a compressed name has a meaningful root. Test the resulting word rather than assuming the original meaning survives the compression.

Do not mechanically remove vowels.

Do not create fake-SaaS gibberish solely to obtain a domain.

The result should still have an intuitive reading or a deliberate, teachable pronunciation.

## Phase 4: generate broadly

Generate far more ideas internally than will ever be shown.

Explore enough distinct roots and constructions to support meaningful comparison. Do not use hundreds of names or any other arbitrary volume as a proxy for research quality. Track actual roots investigated, raw names generated, names pitched, rejected names, and screened survivors separately.

Raw ideas are not finalists.

Stay within the selected name types and hard constraints. The following are possible approaches, not a requirement to produce types the user excluded.

Include:

- obvious ideas
- uncomfortable ideas
- lateral ideas
- historical ideas
- foreign-language roots
- metaphorical ideas
- arbitrary names
- compressed forms
- compounds
- coined constructions
- culturally grounded ideas

Do not judge too early during initial generation.

Then begin killing aggressively.

---

## Phase 5: territory review and pivot

After each territory, evaluate the pattern of results.

Ask:

### Are they generic?
Move farther from category terminology.

### Are they all unavailable?
The semantic territory may be too crowded. Move laterally rather than adding random suffixes.

### Do they sound like generic SaaS?
Stop combining technology words and adjectives. Move into metaphor, history, behavior, objects, or culture.

### Are they clever but unreadable?
Reduce linguistic manipulation.

### Are they descriptive but weak?
Move from function to consequence or metaphor.

### Are they meaningful but boring?
Investigate stronger phonetics or less obvious representations of the same idea.

### Are they memorable but irrelevant?
The naming story is too weak. Reconnect to strategy.

### Are we generating variants of the same five roots?
Stop. Create a new territory.

Never respond to a weak naming round by producing 50 cosmetically similar names.

Distinguish a weak territory from a promising root with a weak first construction. Keep source-backed roots and specific rejection reasons. Revisit shelved candidates when new evidence or a better form changes the comparison; do not treat an agent's first readability opinion as a measured audience result.

---

## Phase 6: first knockout

Before expensive/current external research, remove intrinsically weak candidates.

Kill names that fail badly on:

- readability
- pronunciation
- spelling
- memorability
- distinctiveness
- sound
- visual appearance
- brand fit
- strategic relevance
- ability to grow
- obvious negative meaning
- excessive explanation required
- generic SaaS feel
- similarity to competitors
- dependence on a temporary trend

Do not fall in love with a name because of its origin story if the actual word is weak.

---

## Phase 7: fresh external screening

Research must be performed again for every naming project.

Never treat previous search results as permanent facts.

For each surviving candidate, perform current research using the source routes and exact-name checks in [research-sources.md](research-sources.md). Finish accessible checks before presenting recommendations. Reject hard failures, generate replacements, and screen those too. Do not stop at an unchecked list or a plan to research later.

### 1. Existing company/product search

Search exact name and close variants.

Check:

- companies
- products
- software
- apps
- startups
- agencies
- major projects
- entertainment properties where relevant
- active brands
- dead companies that may still own rights
- common industry usage

Search beyond the first result page when needed.

---

### 2. Domain availability

Follow the project's actual domain requirement.

If exact `.com` is mandatory, check the exact `.com`.

Distinguish:

- unregistered
- registered
- parked
- premium
- active company
- available at normal registration price

Never claim a domain is available based on search-engine absence.

Check it live using [domain-checks.md](domain-checks.md), including the public unauthenticated Vercel lookup and other accessible routes before claiming credentials or unavailable tools prevent it.

---

### 3. Preliminary trademark knockout

Search relevant trademark databases/jurisdictions.

Look for:

- exact matches
- close spelling
- phonetic similarity
- related goods/services
- confusingly similar marks

This is a knockout screen, not legal clearance.

Never tell the user a name is legally cleared unless qualified trademark counsel has completed the appropriate search.

---

### 4. Linguistic and cultural review

For relevant markets, investigate:

- meaning
- pronunciation
- slang
- vulgar meanings
- political/religious baggage
- cultural associations
- unintended translations
- confusing soundalikes

For foreign-language-derived names, verify the claimed etymology using reliable linguistic sources.

---

### 5. Search/social usability

Where relevant, examine:

- search-engine ambiguity
- dominant unrelated meanings
- social handle landscape
- hashtag ambiguity
- voice-search issues
- autocorrect problems

---

## Phase 8: deep finalist evaluation

Only names surviving meaningful screening become finalists.

Evaluate finalists on:

- distinctiveness
- strategic fit
- magnetism
- memorability
- pronunciation
- spelling
- euphony
- visual form
- conciseness
- longevity
- category differentiation
- extensibility
- cultural appropriateness
- protectability
- domain usability
- searchability
- story
- emotional associations
- ability to become bigger than its initial explanation

Do not reduce the decision to “Do you like it?”

Evaluate what associations the name creates and what strategic work it performs.

---

## Phase 9: the brand-story test

For every serious finalist, explain:

1. Where the name comes from
2. Why that source relates to the company
3. Why the connection is interesting rather than forced
4. What the name communicates without explanation
5. What additional meaning appears once the story is known
6. Whether the story gives marketing/design teams useful material
7. Whether the name still works if nobody ever hears the story

The story is a multiplier, not life support.

---

## Phase 10: real-world test

Test finalists in actual use:

- “Have you heard of ___?”
- “I work at ___.”
- “Send it through ___.”
- “___ raised a Series A.”
- “___ just launched.”
- app icon
- URL
- email address
- logo/wordmark
- spoken introduction
- press headline
- enterprise procurement conversation

Look for friction.

A name can look excellent in a naming deck and fail in speech.

---

## Presentation rule

Do not show the client hundreds of names.

The broad exploration happens internally.

Present only candidates that survived enough scrutiny to be worth discussing.

For each finalist include:

- Name
- Pronunciation if needed
- Naming territory
- Origin/meaning
- Why it fits
- Current domain status
- Current conflict findings
- Important risks
- Confidence / recommendation reasoning

Do not hide weaknesses.

---

## What to avoid

Do not default to:

- `[category word] + AI`
- `[category word] + Guard`
- `[category word] + Watch`
- `[category word] + Signal`
- random `-ly`
- random `-io`
- random `-ify`
- faux-Latin syllables
- generic five-letter invented SaaS words
- mechanical vowel deletion
- meaningless spelling errors designed only to obtain a domain
- endless synonyms of the user's original keywords
- obvious compounds presented as creative work

These patterns may occasionally produce a valid result, but they are not a naming methodology.

---

## Freshness rule

Every project starts fresh.

Never encode domain availability, trademark findings, company conflicts, handle availability, competitor landscapes, slang meanings, or market usage as timeless facts in this reusable method.

Preserve useful project-specific research as dated evidence with URLs, market/jurisdiction, exact query/name, and limitations. Recheck it for each new project and again before a final launch decision. Store the **research method**, not old search conclusions as permanent rules.

---

## Final legal rule

External searches can eliminate obviously unusable names and identify risk.

They cannot provide final legal trademark clearance.

For a serious launch, finalists should receive professional trademark review in the relevant jurisdictions.

---

## Success standard

The goal is not a name that merely sounds good.

The goal is a name that:

- has strategic reason to exist
- feels distinctive in its market
- is easy enough to use
- can become more valuable as the company grows
- has a compelling explanation when one exists
- survives current availability screening
- does not depend on naming trends
- feels ownable rather than generated

The process should be deep enough that the winning name could not have been reached reliably through a ten-minute brainstorm.

## Research records and handoff

Keep one project-specific record rather than mixing future projects into this reusable method:

1. **Brief:** confirmed facts, preferences, hard constraints, open questions, target markets, domain strategy, relevant trademark jurisdictions and goods/services.
2. **Territory register:** territory, source-backed roots and story, research depth, candidate count if actually tracked, observed weakness, keep/pivot/stop decision and why.
3. **Candidate register:** exact spelling, pronunciation, type, territory, root/source, intrinsic strengths, friction, knockout reason or remaining action.
4. **Screening register:** candidate, check type, exact domain/query/mark, URL/database, actual check date, jurisdiction/class or goods/services when known, observed result, limitation, next action.
5. **Decision record:** ranked recommendation, tradeoffs, chosen name only when the user chooses it, rejected alternatives and reasons, unresolved checks, and next step.

Use statuses such as **raw**, **intrinsic pass**, **screening incomplete**, **screened candidate**, **finalist**, **rejected**, and **user selected**. A name is not a screened finalist when required current checks are missing. Use **unknown** or **not checked** rather than guessing.

For domains, distinguish unregistered from an actual purchasable standard-price registration, premium listing, auction, brokerage, or existing active brand. Record quoted price, currency, term and renewal if observed; do not infer them. A parked page, missing website, search absence, or failed DNS lookup is insufficient evidence of availability.

For trademarks, report the scope of the preliminary search and confusing similarities. Avoid a blanket "trademark available" verdict. Hard disqualifiers cannot be averaged away by a score. Etymology sources establish a word's origin, not its commercial safety.

For pronunciation, spelling, and recall, distinguish an agent's desk assessment from actual human testing. Use spoken/heard-first, spelling, and delayed recall trials when participants are available. Never invent test participants, success rates, or memorability scores.

If live research is unavailable after trying accessible alternatives, finish discovery and supported work, disclose exactly what could not be checked and why, and record remaining work. When the brief is usable, present a small **provisional shortlist, screening incomplete** without requiring another permission round. Put the limitation notice before the names and show unknown checks per candidate. Separate desk exploration from verified research and provisional output from a completed screened recommendation. Do not pretend the result is launch ready or repeatedly ask permission to continue accessible independent work.
