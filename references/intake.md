# Business and naming intake

Use this for new naming projects as an internal coverage checklist. Reuse reliable facts already supplied. Ask **one focused question per message**, wait for the reply, record it, and adapt the next question to what was learned. Do not present this entire checklist or disguise multiple unrelated questions as one. A volunteered answer may resolve several fields at once; do not ask them again.

Start with what is being named when unclear. Then learn the offering and audience, taste, and practical constraints in the order that best resolves uncertainty. Ask for liked/disliked names and why before expensive research. Explain naming types progressively when useful, not as a compulsory eight-category opening lecture. Proceed to the direction-calibration step in `SKILL.md` before lengthy research or bulk domain checks.

## Understand the business

Track coverage across these areas; ask only the next material unanswered question:

1. **Business and industry:** What exactly are we naming: company, product, tool, service, feature, or umbrella brand? Explicitly ask which industry, subsector, and category it is in or serves. Labels such as AI, app, or B2B do not answer the industry question. Describe what it does in plain English and give a concrete example of someone using or buying it. Is it a new business or a rename? For a rename, what should be retained or left behind?
2. **Offering and business model:** What problem does it solve, how does it work, and what changes for the customer? What makes it different from the main alternatives? What is actually being sold, at what positioning or price level, and how is it bought? Skip pricing details only when they cannot affect naming.
3. **People and competition:** Who uses it, who pays, and who must trust it? Which industries or customer segments matter? Who are the main competitors or substitutes? Request links or existing materials when available, but do not require a website for a new business.
4. **Brand and growth:** What three qualities should the name suggest? What should it never suggest? Which existing brand names do you like or dislike, and why? What future products, audiences, or markets must the name accommodate?

Match the research to the naming object. For a company or umbrella brand, investigate portfolio breadth, long-term identity, and relevant corporate peers. For a product/tool, investigate its actual job, users' language, competing products, parent-brand relationship, and phrases people use to open, recommend, or work with it. For a feature, consider discoverability within its parent product. Do not force a tool to sound like a venture-backed company or impose descriptive names on all products. Ask about expansion only to the extent the object actually needs it.

Research public facts independently when useful. A competitor page can establish what the competitor sells; it cannot establish the user's business strategy or preferences.

## Choose name types and practical constraints

Start with ordinary examples: ask for a few names the user likes and what appeals about them. Ask about dislikes in a later question if still unclear. A name alone is not enough: identify whether the appeal is sound, meaning, clarity, length, memorability, or something else. Record exact feedback separately from inferred patterns. Do not reuse the examples' vocabulary or formula as a substitute for exploration.

Use the table below as a reference. When the user needs help, explain two or three relevant contrasting types with plain-English definitions, examples, and tradeoffs, then ask one focused question. Show the full menu only when requested or useful to an explicitly broad comparison. Do not require the user to learn taxonomy, select one type, or approve every category. Accept a ranked mix, example-based direction, or explicit delegation.

A stated preference guides emphasis rather than shutting down internal exploration: briefly compare a few other styles in the background to avoid fixation. Keep explicit presentation exclusions binding. Do not pitch a prohibited type. If the user welcomes alternatives, a clearly labeled wildcard may appear alongside, not instead of, the requested choices.

| Type | Plain-English explanation | Familiar example and why it fits | Main tradeoff |
|---|---|---|---|
| Descriptive | Directly says what the business offers or does. | **General Motors** directly identifies the automotive field. | Immediate clarity; can feel generic or limit expansion. |
| Suggestive / evocative | Hints at a benefit, behavior, or feeling. | **DoorDash** suggests fast delivery to your door. | Connects to the benefit; can resemble other category names. |
| Metaphorical | Borrows an image, object, or system whose qualities parallel the business. | **Amazon** draws on the river's scale to suggest a vast selection. | Rich associations and story; the connection may need explaining. |
| Arbitrary / abstract | Uses an existing word unrelated to the category. | **Apple** is a familiar word that does not describe computers. | Flexible and distinctive in context; the business meaning must be built. |
| Coined / invented | Creates a new word, with meaningful roots or a distinctive sound. | **Kodak** was deliberately invented rather than borrowed from an existing word. | A distinctive verbal identity; spelling and pronunciation need testing. |
| Founder / eponymous | Uses a person's name. | **Ford** uses founder Henry Ford's surname. | Personal identity and heritage; ties the brand to a person. |
| Geographic / cultural | Draws on a place, tradition, or cultural reference. | **Patagonia** draws on a real region and its outdoor associations. | A sense of place or identity; may suggest an origin or limit expansion. |
| Acronym / initialism | Uses letters from a longer phrase. | **IBM** shortens International Business Machines. | Compact; unfamiliar letters can be harder to remember or distinguish. |

These examples illustrate naming mechanisms, not rigid categories or guarantees of success. A name can fit more than one type: a geographic reference can also work as a metaphor. Explain that **Apple uses an existing word**, while **Kodak invents one**; do not collapse arbitrary and coined into a single unexplained "abstract" bucket. Do not assume a famous company's recognition proves a similar new name would be easy to establish.

For historical explanations, use verified origin accounts rather than invented brand stories. Source references for the examples above: [Amazon](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/books-and-authors/books-are-in-our-dna), [Kodak](https://www.kodak.com/en/company/page/george-eastman-history/), [Ford](https://corporate.ford.com/corporate/corporate/articles/history/henry-ford-biography/), [Patagonia](https://www.patagonia.com/stories/culture/community/in-the-place-of/story-20353.html), and [IBM](https://www.ibm.com/investor/help/general-faqs). The DoorDash explanation is an illustrative reading of the name, not a claim about the founder's documented intent.

For a taste question, ask in plain language, for example: "Which names do you like, and what do you like about them?" Record the reasons, not just category labels. When examples do not establish direction, use the 5–7 varied exploratory names described in `SKILL.md`, label their checks as incomplete, and get a reaction before the long research pass.

Explain that industry terminology, historical research, and foreign-language roots are research territories; blends, compounds, and compressed spellings are construction methods. These can support more than one name type. Coined and abstract are not interchangeable.

Resolve these constraints sequentially before deep research and screening. Early direction examples may precede remaining practical details, with unknowns explicit:

- Launch and expansion countries; languages the audience reads and speaks; relevant trademark jurisdictions and intended goods/services.
- Whether exact `.com` is mandatory, other allowed extensions or modifiers, and whether buying an existing/premium domain is acceptable. Ask the purchase budget if resale is acceptable. Record an explicit "undecided" rather than assuming flexibility.
- Required social platforms/handles, if any; one word versus multiple words; length or syllable limits; pronunciation and spelling; acceptable wordplay, abbreviations, and altered spelling.
- Excluded words, associations, languages, naming styles, existing roots, and category clichés. Separate hard exclusions from softer preferences.
- Any parent-brand relationship, existing marks, restricted terminology, or need to work in regulated/professional contexts.

## Completion rule

Record each material area as **answered**, **explicitly open/delegated**, or **unanswered**. Do not assume "not mentioned" means "no preference."

Before the deep research and screened naming round, require:

- The business object, industry/subsector, offering/difference, and audience/buyer.
- Markets/languages, desired impression and exclusions, future scope.
- Example-based taste feedback, a selected type direction, or explicit delegation to explore a mix; record whether calibration is complete.
- Domain requirements and other hard usability constraints, including explicit undecided answers where applicable.

Summarize the naming object, brief, direction feedback, success criteria, and disqualifiers. Proceed without an extra approval round once answers resolve these points. Ask one targeted follow-up at a time when answers are too vague to guide decisions. Do not repeat the entire questionnaire for a pivot or a request to check one existing name.

If the user explicitly requests quick ideas without completing discovery, identify the assumptions and produce only exploratory output. Built-in direction calibration also permits explicitly unchecked examples before screening. Neither mode may be presented as the final researched shortlist.
